\section{Conclusion}
Recalling the reaction equation:
$$\rxnpl$$

In section \ref{sec:analysis} we concluded that the precipitate in the reaction is \ce{BaSO4} and not \ce{AlCl3}. When \ce{AlCl3} was produced separately in a reaction with neither \ce{Ba^2+} nor \ce{SO4^2-}, no precipitate formed. When either of the ions in \ce{BaSO4} were absent in other trials, no precipitate formed either. Therefore \ce{AlCl3} is not the precipitate, and \ce{BaSO4} must be the precipitate.

\subsection{Reasoning}
Using our results, we can establish that \ce{AlCl3} is soluble in water (can attain over 0.1M concentration) and that \ce{BaSO4} has low solubility in water (precipitates before reaching 0.1M concentration).

The reason for this is because of differences in molecular attractive forces. In a soluble salt like \ce{AlCl3}, the attractive forces between the ions and water molecules are stronger than the forces between themselves. This results in large amounts of ions being able to exist in the solution in closer proximity without dissolving.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/alcl.png}
    \caption{\ce{AlCl3} ions in solution. The weak attractive forces between the ions allow them to exist closely together without precipitating.}
    \label{fig:attractive_forces}
\end{figure}

In an insoluble salt like \ce{BaSO4}, the attractive forces between the ions are stronger than the forces between the ions and water molecules. The ions are not able to exist in such close proximity as the \ce{AlCl3} without pulling each other out of the solution and forming a precipitate. While ions still exist in the solution, they are at a much lower concentration and are constantly being pulled in and out of the solution at equilibrium.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/baso.png}
    \caption{\ce{BaSO4} ions in solution. The strong attractive forces between the ions cause them to precipitate out of the solution. The remaining ions must be at a much lower concentration to not precipitate. The ions are in equilibrium, and are constantly being pulled in and out of the solution.}
    \label{fig:attractive_forces2}
\end{figure}

The difference in attractive forces leads to one salt being soluble, and the other having low solubility and undergoing a chemical change to form a precipitate. By substituting ions in the reaction, we can change the attractive forces between ions, and thus change whether a precipitate forms or not. Recording the results of these changes allows us to slowly piece together the solubility table.
